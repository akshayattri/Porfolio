A product story about finding the signal in customer data.

01The challenge / The work between interactions

A CRM holds the data. People carry the context.

When a salesperson opens a customer record, the question is rarely “what data do we have?” It is “what happened, where do we stand, and what should happen next?”

EasyCRM brings leads, communication history, deals, and tasks into a connected workspace. The design challenge was to make each of those views useful on its own while preserving the relationship between them.

The central design question

How might we help a team move from a customer list to a confident next action without piecing the story together across screens?

01

Context is scattered

Contact details, notes, activity, and opportunities answer different questions but need to be understood together.

02

Views serve different jobs

A lead table supports comparison. A timeline supports understanding. A deal board supports tracking progress.

03

Follow-up is the real work

Finding a customer is only the start. The interface needs to make the next useful step discoverable and attributable.

These are design-framing observations based on the product workflows, not quantified user-research findings.

02Discovery / Understand the work

Different responsibilities. One shared record.

The design lens came from mapping the main jobs across the CRM: triage incoming leads, revisit a relationship, manage an opportunity, and make sure a follow-up has an owner.

Workflow review

Break down how a person moves between collections, details, activity, deals, and tasks. Identify what needs to stay visible at each transition.

Information prioritization

Separate persistent identity, chronological context, and actionable work so that one dense screen does not give everything equal weight.

About the user models: The two archetypes below are workflow-based design aids, not personas claimed to come from interviews or a formal research sample.
SR

Sales representative

Primary job · Maintain momentum
“Show me what happened last so I know what to do now.”
Needs

Find a lead quickly, recognize its stage, review recent interactions, and record the next follow-up.

Design response

Searchable collections, an activity-first record, and nearby notes, deals, and tasks.

TL

Team lead

Primary job · See progress and ownership
“Help me see the work that is moving—and the work that needs attention.”
Needs

Understand pipeline distribution, review ownership, and recognize tasks requiring action.

Design response

A consistent deal board, repeatable card anatomy, and a dedicated task workspace.

Information needs

Recognize → Interpret → Act

First identify the person, then read the relationship, then choose the next step. This sequence informed the record layout.

Experience principle

Details without disorientation

Moving into a record should deepen context, not make users lose track of the work they were doing.

04Interaction decisions / One system, several ways of working

Give each view a clear job.

Rather than forcing all CRM work into a universal layout, I used familiar patterns with specific purposes—and repeated information structures wherever consistency reduces the learning cost.

01 / Comparison

Table for finding and triage

Consistent columns make lead identity, ownership, and status easier to compare; filtering and smart lists help users narrow the working set.

02 / Context

Three-zone customer record

Stable identity on the left, chronological activity in the center, and related work to the right distinguish background from decisions.

03 / Progression

Board for opportunity status

Pipeline columns express stage visually, with repeatable cards carrying the details of each opportunity.

04 / Commitment

Task view for follow-through

Due dates, status, ownership, and customer association turn a vague next step into trackable work.

Decision: let the timeline lead.

The activity stream carries the changing story of the relationship. Giving it visual priority supports reading without making the static profile disappear.

Tradeoff: A richer record increases information density. Stable zones, labels, and separation of concerns keep that density purposeful.

07Delivery / Reflection and the next iteration

Less searching for context. More space to act.

The design outcome is a common record model across leads, activities, opportunities, and tasks, with each workspace optimized for the decision it supports.

This case study documents design structure and visible product work. It does not claim a measured uplift in conversion, task completion, or team productivity.

What the work reinforced

  • ClarityGive identity, history, and next actions separate visual roles so a dense page remains legible.
  • ConsistencyKeep card anatomy, status language, and common actions recognizable as users change workspaces.
  • ContinuityAllow a person to move from a collection to the customer record and back into coordinated work.

What I would validate next

Run a task-based usability study: ask participants to locate an unfamiliar lead, explain the last interaction, and assign a follow-up. Observe navigation reversals, incorrect interpretations of status, and whether the relationship context survives the move into tasks.
